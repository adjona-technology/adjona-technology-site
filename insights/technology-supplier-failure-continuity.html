When your technology supplier disappears

29 October 2026
6 min read
Adjona Technology

A technology provider's collapse has disrupted online services at hundreds of GP practices across England and Wales. The incident highlights a risk that charities, care providers and smaller organisations cannot afford to overlook: what happens when a critical supplier can no longer deliver?

When a supplier failure becomes a service failure

In October 2026, hundreds of GP practices experienced disruption to their websites and online patient services after technology supplier Silicon Practice, operated by Schappit Ltd, entered administration. The affected services included practice websites, online consultation facilities and chatbot functions. Patients were directed to alternative contact arrangements, including telephone and in-person access, while practices worked to maintain services.

The incident is not being reported as a cyberattack. It demonstrates a different but equally important category of operational risk: dependence on an external technology provider whose services may become unavailable through business failure.

For organisations increasingly reliant on hosted websites, cloud platforms, booking systems, case management applications and outsourced IT services, the implications extend well beyond healthcare.

A system can be technically reliable and still represent a significant business continuity risk. The question is not simply whether a supplier operates securely and maintains backups. It is whether the organisation can continue delivering essential services if that supplier suddenly stops operating.

Five questions worth answering before a supplier fails

1. Who actually controls your critical systems?

An organisation may pay for a website, booking platform or managed service without controlling the infrastructure needed to keep it running.

Consider who holds administrative access to the domain registrar, DNS configuration, hosting environment, cloud tenant and associated service accounts. If a supplier becomes unavailable, can an authorised representative of the organisation access these systems independently?

Control should not depend entirely on the goodwill, availability or continued existence of one provider. For critical systems, organisations should establish clear ownership, maintain appropriately protected emergency administrative access and document how control could be transferred.

2. Could you retrieve your data and move to another provider?

Having a contractual right to your data is not the same as being able to retrieve and use it when necessary. An organisation may have access to reports or downloadable files but lack the configuration information, supporting records or documentation needed to reconstruct the service elsewhere.

It is worth establishing:

  • What data can be exported, in which formats and how frequently?
  • Who is responsible for maintaining recoverable copies?
  • Would the exported information be usable by a replacement provider?
  • Are configuration settings, integrations and dependencies documented?
  • What happens to data access and retention if the supplier ceases trading?

These questions are particularly important where systems hold personal information, case records, care documentation or other operationally sensitive data. A backup has limited value if nobody knows how to restore it into a working service.

3. What happens to people who depend on the service?

The consequences of a technology outage are not limited to unavailable software. When an online service fails, people may be unable to submit requests, obtain information or communicate with the organisation. Staff may need to manage additional telephone calls, process requests manually or explain delays.

For a charity, this could mean beneficiaries losing access to referral forms or support requests. For a care provider, it could affect staff access to operational information. For a small business, it could prevent customers from making enquiries or bookings.

A continuity plan should therefore identify the service being delivered, not just the technology supporting it. Can the organisation provide an alternative contact method? Is there a manual process? Who informs service users? How will additional work be managed? The objective is to preserve essential service delivery, even when the preferred technology is unavailable.

4. Does your contract cover an orderly exit?

Supplier contracts are often reviewed for costs, service levels, security obligations and renewal dates. Exit arrangements deserve similar attention.

Before relying on a supplier for an essential service, organisations should understand what assistance would be available if they needed to change providers, what data would be returned and whether migration support is included. Important considerations include notice periods, data export rights, subcontractor dependencies, termination arrangements and the ownership of technical assets.

However, contractual protections have limits. A supplier facing insolvency may not have the resources to provide the assistance originally promised. An effective exit plan should therefore avoid relying exclusively on the supplier's ability to cooperate at the point of failure.

5. Have you tested the arrangements?

A business continuity document may describe alternative processes without establishing whether they would work. A straightforward exercise can reveal significant gaps.

Ask the relevant staff to consider a scenario in which a critical supplier becomes unavailable tomorrow morning. Who would be contacted first? Could administrators access the necessary systems? Where are the backups? Could staff explain alternative arrangements to service users? What would be required to restore the service through another provider?

The exercise does not have to be elaborate. Even a short, structured review can identify undocumented dependencies, unclear responsibilities and unrealistic assumptions. The outcome should be a manageable action list, with named owners and agreed review dates.

What trustees and managers should expect to see

For charities and other organisations with limited internal IT resources, supplier resilience should form part of ordinary governance rather than becoming a separate technical exercise.

A useful starting point is a register of critical technology suppliers, identifying the services each provides, the organisation's dependency on them and the arrangements available if those services are disrupted. Trustees and senior managers do not need to understand every technical component. They should, however, be able to obtain clear answers about ownership, recoverability, alternative processes and accountability. For higher-risk services, those answers should be supported by evidence rather than informal assurances.

The aim is proportionate oversight. A small charity does not need the same continuity infrastructure as a large healthcare organisation, but it still needs to know whether essential services can continue.

Business continuity is about retaining control

The Silicon Practice incident is a timely reminder that operational resilience depends on more than cybersecurity or system availability. Supplier financial difficulties, commercial disputes, unexpected closures and loss of technical support can all threaten access to essential services.

Organisations cannot eliminate every supplier risk. They can, however, reduce their exposure by understanding their dependencies, retaining appropriate control over important assets, maintaining usable data and preparing alternative ways of working.

The most useful question to ask today may be a simple one: if this supplier disappeared tomorrow, what could we still operate ourselves? That is where a practical business continuity review should begin.

Your IT is outsourced. Your responsibility is not.

August 2026
7 min read
Adjona Technology

Outsourcing IT can be a sensible decision. A charity gains specialist support without employing a technical team. A care provider gets help maintaining essential systems. A small business can concentrate on its customers.

But paying someone to manage your technology does not answer every question about how it is protected.

Who can access your information? Who approves changes? What happens if the supplier cannot operate? Could you regain control of your accounts if the relationship ended tomorrow?

These are governance questions, not just technical ones.

The National Protective Security Authority's guidance for suppliers encourages organisations to demonstrate their security posture through Cyber Essentials or equivalent assurance and to share relevant security information with customers. For customers, the practical lesson is to ask for evidence rather than rely entirely on reassurance.

Responsibility needs to be shared, not assumed

A supplier has responsibilities of its own. Outsourcing does not make it exempt from contractual commitments or applicable legal duties. Equally, appointing a supplier does not remove the customer organisation's need for oversight.

Where an organisation is the controller of personal information and a supplier processes it on its behalf, the Information Commissioner's Office explains that the relationship requires appropriate contractual arrangements and continuing oversight. Processors also have their own obligations.

The objective is not to duplicate the supplier's job. It is to agree who does what, identify what remains your responsibility and obtain enough evidence to make informed decisions.

We recommend starting with five questions.

1. Who has administrator access?

An IT supplier may need powerful access to manage Microsoft 365, devices or business applications. That access should be identifiable, justified and reviewable.

Ask which supplier accounts can administer your systems, what permissions they hold and how those accounts are protected. Check whether access belongs to named individuals or relies on shared credentials, and how it is removed when a supplier's employee leaves.

In Microsoft 365, ask the supplier to explain both administrator accounts within your tenant and any delegated partner access. Request a dated summary of permissions and an agreed review schedule.

Your organisation should also have a secure route to recover administrative control without depending entirely on one supplier or individual. This does not mean giving every trustee or manager administrator rights.

2. Where does your information go?

Information can move beyond its original system during routine support. A screenshot may be attached to a ticket. A file may be downloaded for troubleshooting. Diagnostic logs may be sent to another provider.

Ask what the supplier can access, where support copies are stored, who else can see them and when they are deleted. Include subcontractors, remote-support platforms and AI tools in that discussion.

For a charity or care provider, an ordinary-looking support request could contain sensitive details about beneficiaries, residents or staff. Agree how such information should be minimised or redacted before it is shared.

A confidentiality statement is useful, but it should sit alongside a clear description of how information is handled in practice.

3. Who tells you when something goes wrong?

An incident may begin with a compromised supplier account, a missing device or an unavailable service. Your organisation needs timely information even if the investigation is incomplete.

Agree an incident contact, an alternative communication channel and an escalation process. Establish what the supplier must report, how quickly it must notify you and how further updates will be provided.

Separate the supplier's notification to your organisation from any decision to notify regulators or affected people. Those decisions depend on the circumstances and the applicable obligations. Do not assume the supplier will make every decision or submit every report for you.

Keep essential contact details somewhere accessible if your usual email and document systems are unavailable.

4. What happens if the supplier stops operating?

Supplier risk is not limited to someone stealing information. An outage can interrupt the services you depend on even when your own systems remain available.

In its 26 August 2026 disclosure, Boston Scientific reported that a cybersecurity incident had disrupted global operations, including its ability to process and ship customer orders. At the time of the filing, the restoration timeline and full impact were unknown. The disclosure did not establish that medical devices themselves had been compromised.

This is an illustration of operational dependency, not evidence of negligence or a particular technical cause.

For your organisation, identify which suppliers are essential, what would stop working without them and how long you could manage. Consider alternative contacts, temporary working arrangements and access to critical records.

Where backup or recovery is included in a contract, ask what is covered and when restoration was last tested. A backup promise and a demonstrated recovery are not the same thing.

5. Can you leave without losing control?

An exit plan should be agreed before a relationship becomes difficult.

Check who controls your domain names, Microsoft 365 tenant, subscriptions, website and essential applications. Confirm how information would be exported, how another provider would take over and what assistance or charges would apply.

Include automations and integrations. A workflow may depend on an account, connection or secret that only the departing supplier controls. Removing access without transferring those dependencies could interrupt services.

The handover should cover documentation, necessary credentials transferred securely, replacement access, revocation of old permissions and agreed handling of retained copies. Test that your organisation has regained control rather than assuming a handover email completes the process.

Five questions to ask before you rely on reassurance

Who has administrator access, and how is it reviewed and removed?
Where does your information go during routine support, and who can see it?
Who tells you when something goes wrong, and how quickly?
What happens to your services if the supplier stops operating?
Could you leave the relationship without losing control of your own systems?

Keep the evidence proportionate

A small organisation does not need a lengthy procurement exercise for every supplier. Focus effort on those with sensitive information, privileged access or a critical role in service delivery.

A simple supplier register can record the service owner, access granted, evidence reviewed, incident contacts, renewal date and outstanding actions. Review it when the service changes, after an incident and at an agreed interval.

Certificates and insurance can contribute to assurance, but check their scope and validity. Neither replaces a clear agreement about the service your organisation actually receives.

Know what you have delegated

Good outsourcing should give an organisation access to expertise without leaving it dependent on unexplained arrangements.

The most useful question is not simply, "Do we have an IT provider?" It is, "Can we show what our provider manages, what remains ours to do and what happens when something changes?"

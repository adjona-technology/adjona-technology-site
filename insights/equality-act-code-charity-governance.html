What the draft Equality Act Code of Practice means for charity governance

August 2026
6 min read
Adjona Technology

The Equality and Human Rights Commission’s 2026 Code of Practice for Services, Public Functions and Associations was published on GOV.UK in July 2026. On 21 July 2026, the Charity Commission encouraged charities to begin preparing for compliance rather than waiting for its updated guidance.

While the final Code has not yet come into force, the draft is a timely reminder that governance is about more than having the right policies. It is also about being able to demonstrate how decisions were made, who approved them and how they are implemented. Now is a good opportunity for trustees and senior leaders to review how policies are managed and how governance decisions are recorded.

This article is not legal advice. It considers the practical governance questions charities should be asking, and how good information management can help organisations prepare.

Why this matters

Many charities already have equality, diversity and inclusion policies. They may also have complaints procedures, volunteer guidance and service standards.

The challenge is often not whether these documents exist, but whether the organisation can demonstrate:

  • which version was approved
  • when it was approved
  • who approved it
  • what information trustees considered
  • whether professional advice informed the decision
  • when the policy is due for review

If those records are scattered across email inboxes, personal drives and shared folders, answering those questions can become difficult.

Governance is about evidence

Trustees are responsible for directing the charity and acting in its best interests. Good governance means being able to evidence those decisions.

For example, if a policy is questioned in the future, could your charity quickly locate:

  • the trustee meeting where it was approved
  • the papers circulated before the meeting
  • any legal or specialist advice received
  • earlier versions of the policy
  • the current approved version
  • evidence that staff or volunteers received updated guidance

These are practical governance questions rather than purely legal ones.

Areas charities may wish to review

The publication of the draft Code provides an opportunity to review:

  • equality, diversity and inclusion policies
  • service delivery procedures
  • complaints processes
  • volunteer and staff guidance
  • trustee decision-making records
  • policy approval processes
  • document version control
  • scheduled policy review dates
Even where no immediate policy changes are required, ensuring that governance records are complete and well organised will make future reviews significantly easier.

Using Microsoft 365 to strengthen governance

Many charities already subscribe to Microsoft 365 but use only a fraction of its governance capabilities. A well-designed Microsoft 365 environment can help organisations:

  • maintain a controlled policy library in SharePoint
  • keep version history for governance documents
  • restrict editing while allowing staff to read approved policies
  • record approvals and document ownership
  • schedule policy review reminders
  • provide trustees with a secure location for board papers
  • maintain an audit trail of changes

Technology does not replace good governance, but it can make governance easier to manage and easier to demonstrate.

Questions for trustees

The draft Code provides a useful opportunity for trustees to ask:

  • Can we identify every policy that may be affected?
  • Do we know which version is the current approved version?
  • Can we show when and why policies were approved?
  • Are review dates recorded?
  • Are governance records stored in a consistent location?
  • Could we quickly provide evidence of our decision-making if asked?

If the answer to several of these questions is uncertain, now is a good time to review how governance information is managed.

Final thoughts

The draft Equality Act Code of Practice is likely to prompt many charities to review their policies over the coming months. Alongside those policy reviews, charities should also consider the governance processes that support them.

Well-managed governance records do more than satisfy regulatory expectations. They reduce organisational risk, improve trustee handovers and make it easier to demonstrate how important decisions were reached.

Preparing now is not simply about complying with future guidance. It is about ensuring that your charity's governance is organised, transparent and supported by clear evidence.

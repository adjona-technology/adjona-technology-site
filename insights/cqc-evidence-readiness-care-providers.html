CQC is changing how it assesses care providers: is your evidence ready?

August 2026
10 min read
Adjona Technology

The Care Quality Commission is changing the way it assesses health and social care providers in England.

Following criticism of its Single Assessment Framework, CQC has developed four draft sector-specific assessment frameworks covering adult social care, hospitals, primary and community care, and mental health services. Its consultation on the draft frameworks closed in June 2026. CQC says the familiar five key questions — Safe, Effective, Caring, Responsive and Well-led — will remain, but its proposed approach reintroduces structured assessment questions and rating characteristics tailored to individual sectors.

For care providers, however, the important issue is not simply what the framework is called.

It is whether the organisation can produce reliable evidence of how it operates when CQC asks for it.

For charities, CICs and smaller independent care providers, that makes information management a governance issue as much as a technology issue.

This article provides practical information-management and digital-governance guidance. It is not CQC registration, regulatory or legal advice.

CQC regulation is changing again

CQC's current assessment approach continues to use its five key questions and quality statements. At the same time, the regulator is developing sector-specific frameworks intended to make assessments more relevant to different parts of health and social care.

The proposed frameworks retain:

  • Safe
  • Effective
  • Caring
  • Responsive
  • Well-led

But CQC has proposed replacing the current quality statements with more structured questions, similar in principle to the previous Key Lines of Enquiry, and reintroducing descriptions of what different rating levels look like in each sector.

That transition is important, but providers should be cautious about redesigning their entire compliance systems around a draft framework.

The underlying regulatory standards have not disappeared. CQC has made clear that its assessment frameworks continue to link back to the fundamental standards established under health and social care legislation.

The practical priority therefore remains the same:

Can your organisation demonstrate that its policies, processes and day-to-day practice match the standards it says it follows?

Evidence cannot be created the night before an inspection

Historically, some organisations treated regulatory inspection as an event. Documents were reviewed, folders assembled and staff briefed shortly before inspectors arrived.

That is an increasingly fragile approach.

CQC can draw on different types of information about a provider, including people's experiences, feedback from staff and leaders, information from partners, observations, organisational processes and outcomes. Under the existing framework these have been formalised as evidence categories.

Whatever terminology eventually appears in CQC's final sector-specific frameworks, providers still need reliable evidence behind their services. For example:

  • Are safeguarding concerns recorded and followed through?
  • Can management demonstrate that incidents resulted in appropriate action?
  • Are staff training records current?
  • Are policies reviewed when they should be?
  • Can the provider demonstrate that risks identified in audits resulted in improvement?
  • Are complaints recorded alongside outcomes and learning?
  • Can managers identify the current approved version of an important policy?
  • Is there evidence that actions agreed at management meetings were actually completed?

The problem for many smaller providers is not necessarily that this information does not exist. It is that nobody can find all of it quickly and confidently.

The danger of the compliance folder

A large shared folder containing hundreds of Word documents is not the same thing as an evidence-management system. Common problems include files called:

  • Safeguarding Policy FINAL.docx
  • Safeguarding Policy FINAL NEW.docx
  • Safeguarding Policy 2026 final2.docx
  • Safeguarding Policy USE THIS ONE.docx

The consequences are more serious in a regulated care environment than simple administrative inconvenience. Staff may follow obsolete guidance. Managers may submit the wrong evidence. Documents can have no identifiable owner or review date. Important actions can remain buried in meeting minutes. Evidence may sit in the mailbox of somebody who has left.

Good digital governance should answer four basic questions about important evidence:

What is it? Who owns it? Which version is authoritative? When must something happen next?

Registration is also becoming less forgiving

Evidence quality matters before a provider even begins delivering regulated services.

CQC's current registration guidance says applications are checked for completeness, accuracy and the correct supporting documentation. If minimum requirements are not met, the application cannot proceed and must be corrected and submitted again.

CQC's June 2026 supporting-document guidance goes further. It says applications can be rejected where required documents are missing, contain incorrect or outdated information, fail to include required information, or are not relevant to the proposed service and regulated activities.

Its current provider-registration guidance states plainly:

“We will reject any incomplete applications.”

For a new care provider, poor document control can therefore delay the organisation before it has even opened its doors.

A registration evidence workspace should not be assembled at random from attachments in different email chains. There should be a controlled location containing the required policies, statements, supporting evidence, ownership information and submission status.

What good evidence management looks like

Care providers do not necessarily need another specialist system for every administrative problem. Many already licence Microsoft 365 and can use capabilities they have to build a more controlled evidence environment.

A practical structure might contain several connected components.

A controlled policy library

A SharePoint document library can provide a single location for approved policies. Documents can carry metadata such as:

  • policy owner
  • service area
  • approval date
  • approving person or body
  • next review date
  • policy status
  • related regulatory requirement

Version history means that previous versions can be retained without staff having to create filenames such as FINAL-V7. Access can also be designed so that a small number of authorised people edit policies while a wider workforce sees only the approved material.

Evidence registers rather than spreadsheets everywhere

Microsoft Lists can be useful for structured information that would otherwise become multiple disconnected Excel files. For example, a provider might maintain registers for:

  • policy reviews
  • incidents
  • complaints
  • improvement actions
  • audits
  • staff training
  • risks
  • regulatory evidence requests

The objective is not to create databases for their own sake. It is to make ownership and status visible. A manager should be able to see:

What is overdue? What remains unresolved? Who owns it?

Automated reminders

A policy should not become obsolete simply because somebody forgot to put a date in their diary. Power Automate can monitor review dates and send reminders before action becomes overdue. The same approach can support:

  • expiring evidence
  • mandatory document reviews
  • outstanding audit actions
  • management follow-ups
  • recurring governance checks

Automation does not remove managerial accountability. It helps make that accountability harder to forget.

Management evidence should connect to action

Meeting minutes often demonstrate that managers discussed a problem. They do not necessarily demonstrate that the problem was fixed. An action register can record:

  • the issue identified
  • the agreed action
  • the responsible person
  • the target date
  • current status
  • supporting evidence
  • completion date

This creates a much clearer trail from:

problem → decision → action → evidence → outcome

That is useful for regulatory assurance, but it is equally valuable for running the organisation properly.

Do not build your Microsoft 365 environment around CQC alone

There is an important trap here. An organisation can become so focused on demonstrating compliance that it builds an elaborate electronic filing structure designed solely around a regulator. That is rarely good information architecture.

The evidence CQC may need should normally emerge from the organisation's ordinary management processes.

For example, a safeguarding record exists because the organisation needs to safeguard people, not because CQC might inspect it. An incident review exists because management needs to understand what happened and prevent recurrence. A training record exists because the provider needs competent staff.

The digital environment should support those operational purposes first. Regulatory evidence should be a consequence of well-managed services, rather than a parallel administrative exercise.

Questions care providers should ask now

The transition in CQC's assessment approach provides a good opportunity for boards, trustees, registered managers and senior leaders to test their evidence arrangements. Ask:

  1. Can we find the current approved version of every critical policy?
  2. Does every important document have an identifiable owner?
  3. Do we know which policies or evidence are overdue for review?
  4. Can we demonstrate how incidents and complaints resulted in learning or improvement?
  5. Can we see outstanding actions across the organisation?
  6. Are important records sitting in personal email accounts or individual OneDrive folders?
  7. Could another manager understand the evidence if the current registered manager suddenly left?
  8. Can we control who sees sensitive evidence?
  9. Can we show when important documents changed and who changed them?
  10. Could we respond to a CQC evidence request without spending days searching through folders and email?

If several answers are uncomfortable, the problem may not be the amount of evidence being collected. It may be how that evidence is managed.

Evidence readiness is also business resilience

There is another reason to address this before an inspection. Good evidence management reduces dependence on individual members of staff.

A registered manager leaving should not take the organisation's operational memory with them. A trustee should not need to search six years of email to understand why an important decision was made. A compliance officer should not be the only person who knows where regulatory evidence is stored.

Well-structured information improves:

  • management handovers
  • accountability
  • business continuity
  • audit preparation
  • board oversight
  • regulatory responses
  • day-to-day decision-making

Those benefits remain even if CQC changes its assessment methodology again.

Final thoughts

Care providers should keep watching CQC's transition towards its new sector-specific assessment frameworks. But organisations do not need to wait for the final framework before improving how they manage evidence.

The fundamental question is much simpler:

If somebody asks you to demonstrate how your service is being safely and effectively managed, can you produce reliable evidence quickly?

Technology cannot make a poorly governed service compliant. But a well-designed Microsoft 365 environment can make good governance considerably easier to operate, monitor and demonstrate.

For charitable and smaller care providers, that may be one of the most useful preparations they can make.

The EU's AI transparency rules now apply. What UK organisations should do.

August 2026
8 min read
Adjona Technology

From 2 August 2026, important transparency provisions of the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act began to apply. The rules cover situations including people interacting with AI systems, the technical marking of AI-generated material, deepfakes and certain AI-generated text published on matters of public interest.

This does not mean that every document edited with AI must carry a prominent warning. The obligations depend on the type of AI system, the content being produced and whether an organisation is acting as a provider or deployer of that system.

For organisations already using generative AI in marketing, communications, customer services or internal workflows, the practical question is now: do we know when and how our use of AI should be disclosed?

What do the transparency rules cover?

Article 50 of the EU AI Act sets out several distinct transparency requirements.

People must know when they are interacting with AI

Providers of AI systems designed to interact directly with people must ensure that users are informed that they are interacting with AI, unless this would already be obvious to a reasonably informed person. A chatbot should not be designed to leave someone believing that they are communicating with a human member of staff when they are not.

AI-generated outputs must support detection

Providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, images, video or text must ensure that outputs are marked in a machine-readable format and are detectable as artificially generated or manipulated. The Act recognises that these technical measures must be proportionate and technically feasible, and contains exceptions for standard editing tools that do not substantially change the original content.

This technical responsibility will normally rest with the organisation providing the AI system rather than an ordinary business using a commercial AI tool.

Deepfakes must be disclosed

Organisations using AI to generate or manipulate images, audio or video that constitute deepfakes must disclose that the material has been artificially generated or altered. For clearly artistic, fictional, satirical or creative works, the disclosure can be provided in a way that does not interfere unnecessarily with the audience's experience.

This is especially relevant to realistic AI presenters, reconstructed events, synthetic voices and videos that show apparently real people, locations or situations.

Some AI-generated public-interest text must be disclosed

The rules also cover AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public about matters of public interest. However, disclosure is not required where the material has undergone human review or editorial control and a person or organisation accepts editorial responsibility for its publication.

Using AI to assist with research, structure or drafting is not necessarily the same as publishing unreviewed AI output. Organisations should still have a named person responsible for checking accuracy, context, tone and suitability before publication.

What does this mean for UK organisations?

The UK does not currently have a general legal requirement that all AI-generated content must be labelled. However, being based in the UK does not automatically place an organisation outside the EU AI Act.

The Act can apply to providers and deployers based in countries outside the EU where the output produced by the AI system is used within the European Union. A UK organisation should examine whether it:

  • provides services to EU customers or beneficiaries
  • operates websites, campaigns or digital services directed at EU audiences
  • deploys AI-generated material as part of work delivered in the EU
  • provides or integrates AI systems for EU-based clients
  • uses realistic synthetic video, audio or images in public communications
Simply assuming that the rules are irrelevant because an organisation is registered in the UK would be unsafe. The precise legal position will depend on how and where the system and its outputs are used.

Platform disclosure rules still matter

Legal compliance is only one part of the picture. YouTube requires creators to disclose realistic content that has been meaningfully altered or synthetically generated, including content that makes a real person appear to do something they did not do.

A piece of content might therefore require disclosure under a platform's terms even where an organisation believes a particular legal exemption applies. Organisations need to check both applicable legislation and the rules of the platform on which the material will be published.

Six practical actions to take now

1
Record where AI is being used
Create a simple register covering AI tools used for written content, images, video, synthetic presenters, audio, chatbots and automated decisions.
2
Separate assistance from autonomous publication
Record whether AI is being used to suggest ideas, edit human-written material or produce complete outputs. The greater the role played by AI, the stronger the review and disclosure process should be.
3
Assign editorial responsibility
Every public-facing item should have a named human owner who checks factual accuracy, permissions, confidentiality, tone and potential harm. "AI produced it" is not an accountability model.
4
Introduce a disclosure standard
Decide what wording the organisation will use — for example: "This video contains scenes generated using artificial intelligence." The wording should reflect what actually happened rather than using a generic disclaimer on everything.
5
Check platform settings before publishing
Where a platform asks whether content is altered or synthetically generated, answer accurately and use the platform's formal disclosure control. Do not rely only on a note in the description.
6
Update the organisation's AI policy
An AI policy should explain which tools staff may use, what information must not be entered into them, who reviews AI-generated work, when disclosure is required, who approves realistic synthetic media and how complaints or mistakes will be handled.

Transparency should become part of normal governance

The objective is not to place an "AI-generated" label on every corrected sentence or resized image. The objective is to prevent people from being misled and to ensure that organisations remain accountable for what they publish.

Charities, community organisations and smaller businesses may not have dedicated AI compliance teams. They can still establish proportionate controls by recording their tools, assigning responsibility, reviewing public content and creating a clear disclosure process.

AI governance does not have to begin with a large compliance programme. It can begin with one straightforward question:

Could a reasonable person be misled about who or what created this content? When the answer might be yes, transparency should be the default.

An AI policy cannot govern tools your organisation has not identified

September 2026
10 min read
Adjona Technology

Many organisations are developing policies to control how artificial intelligence should be used. They are deciding which information employees may enter into AI services, when human review is required and which activities should remain prohibited.

This is a sensible step, but the policy may be starting too late.

Before an organisation can govern AI effectively, it needs to establish which tools are already being used, who is using them, what information they can access and whether any of them can act on the organisation's behalf. Without that visibility, an AI policy may describe the organisation's intended environment rather than the one that actually exists. Good AI governance for charities starts with knowing what is actually in use, not with the policy document itself.

AI adoption does not always follow a formal project

AI is not necessarily introduced through a planned technology programme.

A member of staff may use a public chatbot to improve an email. A volunteer may summarise meeting notes using a personal account. A manager may activate an AI feature already included in an existing application. A team may adopt a transcription service without considering where recordings and transcripts are stored.

Each decision may appear small and reasonable. Collectively, however, they create an environment that the organisation may not fully understand.

This is particularly likely in charities and smaller organisations. People are often trying to improve services, respond to increasing demand and complete administrative work with limited time and resources. A readily available AI tool can appear to provide an immediate solution.

The issue is not necessarily irresponsible behaviour. Informal adoption can simply move faster than governance.

Begin with discovery, not prohibition

An organisation's first response should not be to prohibit every AI tool it has not formally approved.

An immediate ban may drive usage further out of sight, particularly if staff believe that the tools help them complete necessary work. It can also prevent the organisation from learning which problems people are trying to solve.

A better starting point is a structured discovery exercise. The organisation should establish:

  • Which AI tools, assistants and automated services are being used
  • Whether users access them through organisational or personal accounts
  • What work people use them to complete
  • Which categories of information are entered or uploaded
  • Whether the tools retain prompts, files or conversations
  • Which applications are connected to Microsoft 365 or other organisational systems
  • Whether any tool can create, change, send or delete information
  • Who approved the tool and who is accountable for its continued use

The purpose is not merely to compile a software list. It is to understand how AI participates in the organisation's work.

Look beyond obvious AI applications

An AI register should not be limited to well-known chatbots.

AI capabilities increasingly appear inside applications that organisations already use. They may be built into productivity platforms, customer relationship management systems, transcription services, design tools, meeting applications, recruitment systems, fundraising platforms and browser extensions.

Some services may summarise information. Others may generate documents, classify records, recommend actions or perform tasks across several connected systems. The organisation should therefore consider three broad categories.

Standalone AI services. These are tools that users visit directly or access through a dedicated application. Users may enter questions, upload documents or generate text, images, audio, video or code. The principal questions concern account ownership, information handling, retention, supplier terms and human review.

AI features within existing applications. An existing supplier may introduce an AI capability through a product update or licensing change. Users may begin using it without the organisation treating it as a new system. The organisation should confirm whether the feature is enabled, which information it can process and whether existing contracts and risk assessments cover the new capability.

AI agents and automated workflows. An assistant generates information for a person to review. An agent may be able to decide what to do next and use connected tools to complete an action. That distinction matters. A system that can send an email, update a record, create an account or change access permissions presents a different level of risk from one that only produces a draft response.

Understand the connection to Microsoft 365

For many organisations, AI governance is becoming inseparable from Microsoft 365 governance.

An AI application may be able to access email, calendars, Teams conversations, SharePoint sites, OneDrive files or information exposed through another connected application. The quality of the AI policy cannot compensate for weak permissions within those services.

If a user can access information they no longer require, an authorised AI tool may also be able to retrieve or process it. If a SharePoint site is shared too widely, AI could make that information easier to locate and reuse. If former staff, volunteers or suppliers retain accounts, new AI capabilities may increase what those accounts can discover or do.

Before enabling an AI tool, organisations should ask:

  • Does the tool use the permissions of the person operating it?
  • Can it search across email, Teams, SharePoint or OneDrive?
  • Can it access shared mailboxes or confidential sites?
  • Which third-party applications have permission to read Microsoft 365 data?
  • Are administrative accounts separated from everyday user accounts?
  • Are guest users and application permissions reviewed regularly?
  • Can the organisation identify actions performed by an AI-enabled application?
  • Can access be revoked quickly if the tool behaves unexpectedly or an account is compromised?
AI can expose existing governance weaknesses. It does not necessarily create all of them.

Personal accounts create an accountability gap

Personal AI accounts deserve particular attention.

An employee or volunteer may use a personal account because it is convenient, offers features unavailable through the organisation or allows them to continue working without waiting for formal approval.

However, the organisation may then be unable to determine:

  • What information was entered
  • Whether the supplier uses that information to improve its models
  • How long conversations and uploaded files are retained
  • Whether the account is protected by appropriate authentication
  • How information will be retrieved or deleted when the person leaves
  • Whether the organisation can investigate an incident

A policy should explain when personal accounts may and may not be used. The organisation should also provide an approved alternative where there is a genuine and recurring business need. Telling people not to use an unapproved service is more credible when the organisation understands why they were using it and provides a practical route for completing the work safely.

Record who owns each tool

Every approved AI system should have a named organisational owner. Ownership does not mean that the person must understand every technical detail. It means that someone is accountable for ensuring that the tool remains necessary, appropriately configured and subject to review.

The owner should be able to explain:

  • The purpose of the tool
  • The people permitted to use it
  • The information it processes
  • The systems to which it is connected
  • The supplier responsible for providing it
  • The human-review requirements
  • The principal risks and agreed controls
  • The process for reporting a problem
  • The circumstances in which access should be suspended or removed

A system without an owner can remain active long after its original purpose has disappeared.

Distinguish assistance from action

Not every AI use requires the same level of control. An application that suggests alternative wording for a public announcement creates a different risk from an agent that sends the announcement automatically. A tool that summarises a document is different from one that decides whether a beneficiary qualifies for a service.

Organisations should classify AI use according to what the system can do. A proportionate classification might distinguish between:

  • Generating or summarising information for a person to review
  • Recommending a decision that remains subject to human approval
  • Preparing an action that a person must confirm
  • Completing an action automatically within defined limits
  • Making or implementing consequential decisions affecting people, money, services or access

As autonomy and potential impact increase, controls should become stronger. Higher-risk use may require formal approval, testing, detailed logging, clear escalation arrangements and a reliable way to stop the process.

Human review must be meaningful

Policies frequently state that AI-generated information must be reviewed by a person. That instruction is only useful if the reviewer has the time, information and authority needed to identify a problem.

The organisation should define:

  • Which outputs require review
  • Who is suitably qualified to perform it
  • What the reviewer must check
  • Which evidence should be retained
  • When a second approval is required
  • When AI must not be used

A person clicking "approve" without examining the output is not an effective control. Human review is particularly important where AI influences safeguarding, employment, financial, regulatory or service-delivery decisions.

Create a practical AI systems register

An AI systems register does not need to begin as a complex application. A controlled Microsoft List may be sufficient for a smaller organisation. For each tool, the register could record:

  • Tool and supplier name
  • Purpose and business need
  • Organisational owner
  • Approved users or teams
  • Account type
  • Information categories processed
  • Connected systems and permissions
  • Level of autonomy
  • Human-review arrangements
  • Supplier and privacy assessment date
  • Approval status
  • Contract or licence renewal date
  • Last review date
  • Incident and escalation contact
  • Planned retirement date

The register should cover approved, rejected, trialled and discovered-but-not-yet-assessed tools. This creates an evidence trail and prevents the same unsuitable service from being reconsidered repeatedly without reference to the earlier decision.

Include AI in joiner, mover and leaver processes

AI access should be reviewed when somebody joins the organisation, changes role or leaves. Removing a Microsoft 365 account may not remove access to an external service registered with a personal email address. Automated workflows may also continue operating after their original owner has left.

The leaver process should identify:

  • AI services used by the person
  • Workflows, agents or automations they own
  • Connected applications and delegated permissions
  • Prompts, templates or knowledge sources required by colleagues
  • Information that must be transferred or deleted
  • Licences that should be reassigned or cancelled
  • Credentials, tokens and integrations that must be revoked

An automated process should never become ownerless simply because the person who created it has left.

Test the policy against real situations

An AI policy should be tested through practical scenarios rather than approved and filed away. For example:

  • A volunteer uploads beneficiary information to a free AI service
  • A manager uses AI to rank job applicants
  • A transcription application joins a confidential Teams meeting
  • An agent drafts and sends messages from a shared mailbox
  • A supplier introduces AI into an existing platform
  • The owner of an automated workflow leaves unexpectedly
  • An AI-generated report contains an invented fact that influences a trustee decision

Trustees and managers should be able to explain how the organisation would detect, contain and investigate each situation. If the policy does not provide a practical answer, it requires further work.

Governance should enable responsible use

Effective AI governance is not intended to prevent useful innovation. Its purpose is to help an organisation make informed decisions, protect the people whose information it holds and ensure that technology remains accountable to the organisation's objectives.

The foundation is visibility. An organisation needs to know what it is using, why it is using it, what information is involved, who is responsible, what the tool can access or change, how outputs and actions are checked, how access will be removed and what will happen when something goes wrong.

Only then can an AI policy reflect the organisation's real environment.

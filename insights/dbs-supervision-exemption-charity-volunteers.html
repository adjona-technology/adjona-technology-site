The September 2026 DBS change: what charities using volunteers need to do now

August 2026
15 min read
Adjona Technology
From 1 September 2026, the DBS supervision exemption is removed. Charities, community groups, sports clubs and other organisations using volunteers to work with children need to act before the deadline.
Adjona Insights · A discussion of this article

From 1 September 2026, an important change to the definition of regulated activity with children will affect charities, community groups, sports clubs, schools and other organisations that use staff or volunteers to work with children.

The change removes what is commonly known as the supervision exemption. Until now, some people who regularly taught, trained, instructed, cared for or supervised children were not treated as being in regulated activity because they were sufficiently supervised by another person who was already carrying out regulated activity.

From 1 September 2026, that supervision will no longer prevent the role from being classified as regulated activity when the work is carried out frequently enough or overnight. The presence of a teacher, safeguarding lead, head coach or experienced volunteer will no longer provide the same exemption.

For charities that rely heavily on supervised volunteers, this is more than a DBS administration issue. It may require a review of volunteer roles, safeguarding procedures, recruitment records, data protection arrangements and trustee oversight.

What is changing?

Regulated activity is work that a person included on the relevant barred list must not undertake. It is an offence for a barred person to carry out regulated activity with children. It is also an offence for an organisation to knowingly permit a barred person to undertake that activity.

The change affects people who teach, train or instruct children, care for children, or supervise children. The supervision exemption previously meant that some of these activities did not count as regulated activity when the worker or volunteer was sufficiently supervised by somebody else in regulated activity.

From 1 September 2026, the exemption will be removed. A person carrying out one of the relevant activities will be in regulated activity where the frequency or overnight condition is met, even when another suitably checked person supervises them.

The frequency condition is met when the work takes place on more than three days in any 30-day period — meaning at least four days within a rolling 30-day period — or overnight, with the relevant overnight period generally being between 2am and 6am.

An affected person may therefore need an Enhanced DBS check in the child workforce with Children's Barred List information.

Who is most likely to be affected?

Government guidance says that volunteers are the main group affected. Many paid roles involving regular contact with children were already treated as regulated activity. However, some volunteers were previously excluded because they were supervised by a teacher, youth leader, head coach or another person already in regulated activity.

Roles that may need to be reviewed include:

  • volunteers helping children with reading or schoolwork
  • assistant coaches in children's sports clubs
  • supervised volunteers at youth clubs
  • volunteers delivering regular workshops or activities
  • volunteers caring for or supervising children during community programmes
  • volunteers supporting children at faith-based organisations
  • staff or volunteers at outdoor activity centres
  • volunteers accompanying children on overnight trips
  • people regularly mentoring or instructing children

Government examples include a weekly school reading volunteer and a twice-weekly community music club volunteer. Both may become regulated activity from 1 September because the work is carried out frequently enough, even though another person supervises them. An occasional monthly volunteer may remain outside regulated activity under this particular frequency test.

Charities should not assume that frequency is the only relevant consideration. Every role should be assessed according to what the person actually does, where they do it and how frequently they do it.

What is not changing?

The change relates specifically to regulated activity with children. The government has stated that there is no corresponding change to the definition of regulated activity with adults.

It also does not mean that every volunteer who may encounter a child automatically becomes eligible for a barred list check. Eligibility depends on the activities and responsibilities included in the role. Organisations are legally responsible for requesting the correct level of DBS check. They should not request an Enhanced DBS check simply as a precaution when the role is not eligible.

Why charities should act before 1 September

The change comes into force only a few weeks after the government's August announcement. Organisations should not wait until a volunteer's next annual review or until a safeguarding inspection raises the issue.

1
Identify roles in which people teach, train, instruct, care for or supervise children while being supervised by somebody else
2
Check whether those activities are carried out frequently enough or overnight
3
Check whether the person's existing DBS certificate included Children's Barred List information
4
Determine whether a new Enhanced DBS check with Children's Barred List information is required
5
Update recruitment, volunteering and safeguarding procedures
6
Reassess DBS eligibility whenever a person's role or activities change

For many charities, the difficult part will not be understanding the headline change. It will be finding accurate information about what each volunteer actually does. Volunteer records may contain a role title such as "activity helper" or "session volunteer" without explaining whether the person teaches or supervises children, how frequently they attend, or what level of DBS check was obtained.

A practical readiness checklist

Create an inventory of relevant roles. List every paid and volunteer role involving children. Do not rely on job titles — review the activities actually carried out.
Identify roles currently relying on supervision. Find roles that were previously considered not to be regulated activity because the person was supervised. These are most directly affected.
Check the frequency of each activity. Record how many days the activity is performed during a rolling 30-day period. A short session on a particular day still counts as activity on that day.
Review overnight responsibilities. Identify volunteers who accompany children on residential visits, camps or other overnight activities.
Check existing DBS information. For each affected person, confirm the level of check, whether Children's Barred List information was included, the issue date and whether their duties have changed since.
Record the decision and reasoning. The organisation should be able to demonstrate why a role was classified as regulated activity or why it was considered outside the definition.
Update policies and onboarding procedures. Safeguarding policies, safer recruitment procedures, role descriptions, induction checklists, DBS handling policies, privacy notices and volunteer agreements may all need updating.

The data protection issue should not be overlooked

DBS information is highly sensitive. Information about criminal convictions and offences is subject to additional requirements under the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018.

A common mistake is to scan entire DBS certificates and retain them indefinitely in an unrestricted shared folder. DBS handling guidance says certificate information should not be kept for longer than necessary once the relevant decision has been made. Organisations may retain a limited record including the certificate date, name, type of certificate, role, reference number and recruitment decision.

Access to any safeguarding or DBS register should be limited to authorised individuals who genuinely need the information.

Could a spreadsheet be enough?

For a small charity with only a few volunteers, a carefully controlled spreadsheet may appear sufficient. The problem is that spreadsheets can become unreliable when several people edit separate copies, access permissions are too broad, renewal dates are missed, or role changes are not reassessed.

The organisation needs one authoritative record, clearly allocated responsibilities and a reliable method of identifying incomplete or overdue actions.

How Microsoft 365 can support the process

Where an organisation already uses Microsoft 365, a controlled safeguarding and DBS register can be created using Microsoft Lists, SharePoint and Power Automate. A well-designed register might record the person's name, staff or volunteer status, role, relevant activities, frequency, regulated activity assessment, DBS check level, barred list status, next review date and outstanding actions.

Power Automate can notify the safeguarding lead when a new role requires assessment, highlight incomplete information, issue reminders before review dates, escalate overdue actions and produce a summary for trustees — without storing unnecessary information about convictions.

Questions trustees should ask

  1. Which of our roles were previously excluded from regulated activity because the person was supervised?
  2. How do we know how frequently each volunteer works with children?
  3. Have we checked whether existing Enhanced DBS certificates included Children's Barred List information?
  4. Who is responsible for reassessing affected roles?
  5. Can we demonstrate the reasoning behind each eligibility decision?
  6. Are DBS records stored securely and accessed only by authorised people?
  7. Have our safeguarding, recruitment and privacy documents been updated?
  8. How will we know when somebody's duties or working pattern changes?
  9. Are outstanding checks and actions being reported to the appropriate governance body?
  10. Have we obtained specialist advice where eligibility is unclear?

A wider lesson for charity governance

This change demonstrates how quickly a legal or regulatory update can expose weaknesses in an organisation's operational records. A charity may have a safeguarding policy, a DBS procedure and a volunteer handbook, yet still struggle to answer basic questions about who performs particular activities, how frequently they do them and what checks were completed.

Good governance requires working processes that connect policies, people, roles, evidence, responsibilities, review dates and management oversight.

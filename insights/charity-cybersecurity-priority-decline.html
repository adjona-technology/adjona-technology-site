Cybersecurity is slipping down the agenda for small charities

August 2026
5 min read
Adjona Technology

Cybersecurity is receiving less attention from charities at precisely the time when their dependence on digital services is increasing.

The UK Government's Cyber Security Breaches Survey 2025/26 found that 60% of charities considered cybersecurity a high priority for senior management. This was down from 68% in the previous year.

The decline was driven particularly by charities with annual incomes below £100,000. Among these smaller charities, the proportion treating cybersecurity as a high priority fell from 64% to 53%. Only 30% of charities had a trustee or board member with explicit responsibility for cybersecurity.

Only 30% of charities have a trustee or board member with explicit responsibility for cybersecurity, and just 19% have a formal incident response plan.

These figures do not necessarily mean that trustees have stopped caring. Small charities are balancing rising costs, fundraising pressures, increasing service demand, safeguarding responsibilities and limited staff capacity. Cybersecurity can easily appear to be a technical issue that can wait.

However, even a small charity may hold personal information, financial records, safeguarding documents, supporter details and access to online banking. It may also depend heavily on email, cloud storage, social media and third-party platforms to operate.

The answer is not necessarily an expensive transformation programme. Smaller charities need a proportionate foundation built around a few essential controls.

1. Give someone clear responsibility

Cybersecurity should have a named owner, even when the charity has no internal IT team. A trustee does not need to become a technical specialist. Their role is to make sure that appropriate questions are asked, risks are recorded and agreed actions are completed.

Trustees should be able to answer:

  • Who has day-to-day responsibility for cybersecurity?
  • Which trustee provides oversight?
  • When was cybersecurity last discussed by the board?
  • Where are risks, decisions and outstanding actions recorded?
  • Who would coordinate the response to an incident?

Without named responsibility, weaknesses can remain unnoticed because everyone assumes someone else is dealing with them.

2. Protect accounts and administrator access

Email accounts are particularly valuable to attackers. Access to one mailbox can allow someone to impersonate the charity, reset passwords for other services, redirect payments or obtain personal information.

Multi-factor authentication should be required for every user, especially administrators and anyone with access to finance, HR, fundraising or safeguarding information. Charities should also check:

  • How many administrator accounts exist
  • Who owns each account
  • Whether administrators use separate accounts for ordinary work
  • Whether former employees, trustees, volunteers or suppliers still have access
  • Whether shared passwords are being used
  • Which authentication methods are permitted

SMS verification is better than using a password alone, but charities should move towards stronger, phishing-resistant authentication methods such as passkeys where their systems support them.

3. Control access when people join, change roles or leave

Small charities often rely on volunteers, temporary staff, trustees and external advisers. Access may be granted quickly but never reviewed again. A simple joiner, mover and leaver process should record:

  • Which systems and files a person can access
  • Who approved the access
  • Whether access still matches the person's role
  • What must be transferred when the person leaves
  • When accounts and permissions were removed

This does not require a complex system. A controlled Microsoft List or similar register may be sufficient, provided that someone reviews it regularly. The same process should cover shared mailboxes, Teams, SharePoint sites, cloud applications, social media accounts and any automated processes owned by the departing person.

4. Know what is backed up and how it would be recovered

Using Microsoft 365 or another cloud platform does not remove the charity's responsibility for recovery. Trustees should understand:

  • What information is backed up
  • What is protected only by retention or version history
  • How long deleted information can be recovered
  • Who can perform a restoration
  • Whether recovery has ever been tested
  • Whether critical information also sits in third-party systems

A backup should not be treated as reliable merely because a supplier says that one exists. The charity should know what can actually be restored, how long recovery would take and what would happen if an administrator account were compromised.

5. Prepare a basic incident response plan

The government survey found that only 19% of charities had a formal cyber-incident response plan. Just 28% had written guidance identifying whom to notify.

A small charity does not need a large incident manual. A short contact and action sheet can make a substantial difference. It should identify:

  • The person responsible for coordinating the response
  • The IT provider or platform support contact
  • The charity's insurer
  • The Information Commissioner's Office reporting route
  • The Charity Commission's serious-incident reporting route
  • The bank's fraud contact
  • The trustees and senior staff who must be informed
  • Where evidence and decisions will be recorded

The plan should also explain how compromised accounts will be disabled, active sessions revoked and affected people contacted. It must be stored somewhere that remains accessible if the charity's normal email or file system is unavailable.

Cybersecurity should be proportionate, not postponed

The survey found that only 31% of charities had sought external cybersecurity information or guidance during the previous year. Only 13% reported having all five technical control areas associated with Cyber Essentials.

Smaller charities may conclude that formal cybersecurity programmes are beyond their budget. That should not prevent them from establishing basic governance, protecting accounts and preparing for disruption. The first objective is not perfection. It is to know what the charity relies upon, who has access, who is responsible, which risks require attention, and what will happen if something goes wrong.

What to do next

A short, structured review can identify the most important gaps without committing your organisation to unnecessary products or an unaffordable programme of work. Start by naming an owner, checking who holds administrator access, and confirming your joiner, mover and leaver process actually gets followed. Those three steps alone close most of the gaps this year's survey found.

Microsoft 365 for UK charities in 2026
Free licences, discounts and security responsibilities

November 2026
9 min read
Adjona Technology

Microsoft 365 can give UK charities professional email, document storage, collaboration tools and security capabilities at much lower cost. Eligible organisations can access free licences, discounted subscriptions and other nonprofit benefits.

However, changes to Microsoft's nonprofit licensing programme in 2025 mean that some charities may need to reconsider their existing subscriptions. It is also important to distinguish between the cost of a licence and the security capabilities it provides.

A charity may be eligible for free Microsoft 365 licences while still needing stronger identity protection, device management or information governance controls.

Understanding these differences can help trustees, managers and IT administrators make better decisions about technology spending and organisational risk.

1. What Microsoft 365 licences are available to UK charities?

Eligible nonprofit organisations can access several Microsoft 365 offers. Three particularly relevant options for smaller and medium-sized UK charities are Business Basic, Business Standard and Business Premium.

PlanUK nonprofit pricingMain capabilities
Microsoft 365 Business BasicFree for up to 300 eligible usersBusiness email, Teams, SharePoint, OneDrive and web/mobile Office applications
Microsoft 365 Business Standard£2.70 per user/monthBusiness Basic capabilities plus desktop Office applications
Microsoft 365 Business Premium£4.20 per user/monthDesktop applications plus advanced identity, device and security management

Prices are based on Microsoft's published UK nonprofit offers as at November 2026, with annual commitment, excluding VAT. Prices and availability may change.

Microsoft 365 Business Basic: free does not mean limited to email

Business Basic provides considerably more than an email account.

It includes Exchange Online, Microsoft Teams, OneDrive, SharePoint and browser-based versions of Microsoft 365 applications.

For charities that mainly need email, meetings, shared documents and straightforward collaboration, Business Basic may provide much of what they need without ongoing licence costs.

However, it does not include the locally installed desktop versions of Word, Excel, PowerPoint and Outlook that come with Business Standard and Business Premium.

It also lacks several advanced security and device management capabilities included in Business Premium.

Microsoft 365 Business Standard: desktop productivity

Business Standard adds desktop applications, making it suitable for people who regularly work with complex spreadsheets, documents and presentations.

This can be particularly useful for finance teams, administrators and staff whose work depends on full desktop Office functionality.

Business Standard should not, however, be confused with Business Premium. The two plans differ materially in their security and management capabilities.

Microsoft 365 Business Premium: additional security and control

Business Premium combines productivity applications with security and management technologies, including Microsoft Intune, Microsoft Defender for Business, Microsoft Defender for Office 365, Microsoft Entra ID P1 and Microsoft Purview Information Protection.

These capabilities can help organisations apply stronger access policies, manage devices, protect endpoints and email, label sensitive information and respond better to security risks.

The licence makes these capabilities available, but it does not configure them automatically.

An organisation that buys Business Premium without implementing appropriate security policies may get much less protection than the subscription could provide.

2. What changed in Microsoft's nonprofit licensing programme?

Microsoft discontinued two significant nonprofit licence grants at each organisation's first renewal on or after 1 July 2025:

  • The Microsoft 365 Business Premium grant.
  • The Office 365 E1 grant (except in limited Enterprise Agreement scenarios).

As grant subscriptions run for a year, these grant entitlements have now ended for almost all organisations. Charities that relied on them should check what their current arrangements provide.

The Microsoft 365 Business Basic grant remains available for up to 300 eligible users.

Discounted Business Standard and Business Premium subscriptions also remain available.

For charities that previously received Business Premium free of charge, the change has introduced new annual costs.

For example, ten Business Premium licences at £4.20 per user per month would cost £504 a year before VAT.

That may seem modest compared with standard commercial pricing, but for a small charity it is still a cost that should be justified against operational needs and available resources.

More importantly, moving users from Business Premium to Business Basic purely to avoid subscription costs can remove security and management capabilities an organisation relied on.

Charities that have already made that change should check which features they lost and whether anything important now needs to be replaced.

3. Does everyone in a charity need the same licence?

Not necessarily.

A common mistake is to approach Microsoft 365 licensing as though everyone in an organisation has the same requirements.

In practice, different roles may have very different needs.

A member of staff who mainly uses email and Teams may need something different from a finance manager working with complex Excel workbooks.

Likewise, someone responsible for managing organisation-owned laptops or handling sensitive information may need capabilities that a basic productivity subscription does not include.

A role-based licensing assessment can consider:

  • Which applications each person actually uses.
  • Whether locally installed Office applications are needed.
  • Which users and devices need enhanced protection.
  • Whether security policies depend on particular licence entitlements.
  • How staff changes, temporary workers and volunteers are managed.

A mixed licensing approach may be appropriate, as long as each user's licence supports the functionality and security controls their role requires.

There is an important distinction here: assigning Business Premium to a small number of users does not automatically extend all its security entitlements to everyone else.

Licensing decisions should therefore reflect both individual productivity needs and the scope of any organisation-wide security controls.

4. Can volunteers receive free Microsoft 365 licences?

Charities often rely on volunteers, trustees and other unpaid contributors. Their licensing position needs particular attention.

Microsoft distinguishes between nonprofit grants and nonprofit discounts.

Generally, grant licences are restricted to eligible paid employees and unpaid executive staff acting in nonprofit leadership roles. Discounted nonprofit subscriptions may also be available for eligible volunteers.

This means a charity should not assume that every volunteer qualifies for a free Business Basic grant licence.

Some volunteers may need only guest access to particular Teams, SharePoint sites or shared resources rather than a full organisational account.

Others may need their own organisational email address, access to internal applications or wider collaboration capabilities.

The right arrangement depends on the work they do and the access they need.

Whatever licensing arrangement is chosen, the charity should also consider how access is approved, reviewed and withdrawn when someone stops volunteering.

A low-cost or free account can still expose sensitive information if it stays active after the person's involvement has ended.

5. The 85% licence utilisation requirement

Microsoft's nonprofit grants come with another important condition.

Microsoft requires organisations to maintain at least 85% active usage of assigned grant licences.

This is intended to make sure granted subscriptions go to people who genuinely use them.

Microsoft assesses active usage by checking whether users have accessed at least one qualifying cloud workload in the past 90 days.

An organisation with many assigned but unused grant licences may receive notifications and could put its eligibility for future grants at risk.

For charity administrators, this is a practical reason to review licence assignments regularly.

Questions worth asking include:

  • Are licences still assigned to former employees?
  • Have inactive accounts been identified?
  • Are licences being allocated in anticipation of future recruitment without a current need?
  • Are the organisation's usage reports being reviewed?
  • Is responsibility for licence management clearly assigned?

This is not simply about managing costs. It can also reveal dormant accounts and access arrangements that need attention.

6. Free licences do not remove security responsibilities

Microsoft 365 provides the technology through which charities communicate, collaborate and keep information.

The organisation remains responsible for deciding how that technology is governed and used.

For example, a charity might use SharePoint to store safeguarding policies, beneficiary records, financial documents or internal governance papers.

Storing these documents in Microsoft 365 does not, on its own, show that access is appropriately restricted or that retention requirements are being followed.

Similarly, having Microsoft 365 security features available does not show that administrators have configured them correctly.

A proportionate security and governance review should consider several areas.

Identity and access

Multi-factor authentication should be enforced appropriately, administrative access should be limited, and accounts should be reviewed when people join, change roles or leave.

SharePoint and Teams permissions

Information should be accessible to those who need it without unnecessarily exposing confidential material to other staff, volunteers or external users.

Device security

The charity should understand which devices access organisational information and what controls protect them, particularly where personal or unmanaged devices are used.

Information retention and ownership

Policies, records and operational documents should have clear owners. The organisation should know what information it holds, where it is stored and when it should be reviewed or disposed of. It should also know whether important data could be recovered if a subscription ended or an account was lost.

Monitoring and accountability

Security alerts, administrator activity and significant changes should be reviewed by appropriately authorised people.

How complex these arrangements need to be will depend on the organisation's size, how sensitive its information is and the resources available.

A small community charity does not necessarily need the same controls or administrative structure as a large national organisation.

However, both need arrangements that are proportionate to their risks.

7. The trustees' perspective: cost is only part of the decision

For charity trustees, Microsoft 365 licensing may at first look like an operational IT matter.

Yet decisions about the technology used to handle organisational information can have wider implications for financial oversight, risk management and service continuity.

Trustees do not need to become Microsoft 365 administrators.

They should, however, be able to obtain reasonable assurance that:

  • The organisation is making appropriate use of available nonprofit discounts and grants.
  • Licensing arrangements support the work employees and volunteers need to do.
  • Security features are configured to reflect organisational risks.
  • Sensitive information is managed appropriately.
  • Responsibility for ongoing administration and oversight is clear.

The cheapest subscription is not always the most economical choice once the cost of managing risk, maintaining systems or recovering from an incident is taken into account.

Equally, buying more expensive licences does not make up for weak governance or unclear responsibilities.

The aim should be proportionate spending supported by effective controls.

8. Six checks before your next Microsoft 365 renewal

A practical review does not have to be a major technology project.

Before the next renewal, charities can start with six questions:

1. Are we still eligible for our current grants and discounts?

Check nonprofit eligibility, subscription status and renewal dates.

2. Are we paying for licences we do not need?

Review assigned licences, actual usage and role requirements.

3. Are we relying on features that could disappear if we downgrade?

Identify security, compliance and device management dependencies before changing subscriptions.

4. Do our staff and volunteers have appropriate access?

Review licence eligibility alongside account ownership, guest access and leaver processes.

5. Can we demonstrate that our Microsoft 365 environment is managed appropriately?

Look beyond the existence of policies to whether access controls, administrative responsibilities and review procedures are working in practice.

6. Would we see Microsoft's notices, and could we recover without them?

Confirm who receives billing and service notices, that more than one person holds administrative access, and that important data is backed up independently of the subscription.

These checks are a starting point for a better-informed discussion about licensing and governance.

Conclusion: choose licences based on requirements, not price alone

Microsoft's nonprofit programme continues to offer substantial value to eligible UK charities.

Free Business Basic licences and discounted Business Standard and Business Premium subscriptions can help organisations use technology that might otherwise be hard to afford.

However, the changes introduced in 2025 make it worth checking that the arrangements charities moved to still match their needs.

The key is to establish what the organisation needs, who needs access, which capabilities are required and how they will be managed.

Licensing and information governance should be considered together.

A charity that understands both its Microsoft 365 spending and its security responsibilities is better placed to make sustainable technology decisions.
